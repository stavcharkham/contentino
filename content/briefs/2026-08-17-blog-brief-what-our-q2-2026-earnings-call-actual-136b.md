# Blog brief: what our Q2 2026 earnings call actually gives us to write about

A single Slack request asked for a blog post built on the Lemonade Q2 2026 earnings call transcript published by The Motley Fool. That's the whole input. There is no approved brief attached, no pulled quotes, no agreed angle, and no numbers in the request itself. So this brief does two things: it defines the piece we can responsibly write, and it names the evidence we still have to go get from the transcript before a single sentence gets drafted. Earnings-call content sits at the top of our stakes ladder. Anything attributed to an executive, and any figure about growth, loss ratio or profitability, is an attributed public-company claim. That means every sentence in the finished post needs a line-level trace back to the transcript, and the piece needs review before it publishes. It cannot auto-publish. The writable shape is a plain-language explainer: here's what we told investors this quarter, here's what it means if you're a customer or a candidate, here's the link to the full transcript so you can check us. What it isn't is a victory lap assembled from remembered numbers. If a figure isn't in the transcript, it doesn't go in the post. If a quote can't be pasted from the transcript with a speaker name attached, it doesn't go in the post either.

## Why now

The transcript is dated August 7, 2026, and the request landed against it. Earnings commentary has a short useful window: it's most relevant in the weeks after the call and it gets superseded by the next quarter. Writing it now, from the primary transcript rather than secondary coverage, is the accurate and cheap version. Writing it in two months means competing with the next call.

## What changed

Before this request, the earnings call existed only as a transcript on a third-party site. What changed is that someone asked for owned, customer-facing copy built on it, which moves the material from investor-relations territory into brand publishing, and pulls in the review requirements that go with attributed public-company claims.

## Purpose

- Serves: The content team drafting the post, and the legal and investor-relations reviewers who have to sign off on it. Secondarily the communications lead who owns the publishing calendar and needs to know this one has a review dependency.
- Job: Turn one quarter of investor commentary into a plain-language post that a customer or candidate can read without a finance background, with every figure and quote traceable to the published transcript, and with the transcript linked so readers can verify us.
- Metric: Ships with zero unsourced claims flagged in review, and zero post-publication corrections to figures or attributions. Secondary: readers actually click through to the full transcript, which tells us the piece earned trust rather than just summarising.
- Shelf life: Useful for roughly one quarter. It goes stale the moment Q3 2026 results are announced, because the numbers in it stop being current. Refresh triggers: the Q3 call, any restatement or correction to the Q2 figures, or any material company announcement that changes the picture the post describes. If the transcript link moves or the source page changes, fix that immediately rather than waiting for the quarterly cycle.

## Not saying

- We're not saying what the Q2 2026 results were. No revenue, growth, loss ratio, customer count or profitability figure appears in this request, so none appears in this brief.
- We're not saying any executive said anything specific on the call. We have no quoted text from the transcript, so we're not attributing one.
- We're not saying the quarter was strong, improving or ahead of plan. That's a characterisation the transcript has to support, sentence by sentence.
- We're not saying this post can publish without review. Attributed public-company claims are high-stakes by default.
- We're not saying this is a forward-looking piece. Guidance and projections need the source's own hedging language carried forward intact, not paraphrased into confidence.
